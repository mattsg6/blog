At the end of 2025, I picked up a heavy book. It was titled <em>Sentieri, 3rd Edition</em>. In college, I studied Italian and this book was the bible. Most textbooks in the humanities attempt to imbue an upbeat charm in between conjugations and folkloric histories, and this one has a cartoon softness with broad mustachioed faces alongside cohorts of young people navigating micro melodramas.<sup><a id="footnote-1" href="#footnote-1-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;">1</a></sup> I began flipping through the pages, muttering “<em>Io sono Matthew. Ho ventitré anni</em>,” and vigorously enunciating the few expletives I could recall in this mildly familiar tongue. I couldn’t put the book down.

For some years, I wanted to learn a language, ashamed of my American monolinguism and propelled by a lingering sense of stagnation. After college, life settles, the mind grows idle in white collar wastelands, and something slightly torturous is required to make a day meaningfully difficult. I began studying vocabulary using <a href="https://apps.ankiweb.net/" target="_blank">Anki</a>, an open source software where users design their own flashcards, and found an <a href="https://simonepols.com/simple-italian-podcast/" target="_blank">Italian podcast</a> to train my ear. A few months passed and I hit a wall, so I reached out to one of the Italian professors I had at UNC and he connected me with a grad student who was willing to tutor. If I stayed the course, I would be speaking Italian in a matter of days if not hours.

Almost a year has passed and I can confidently say: <em>parlo italiano perfettamente</em>! Such is my proclivity that I only need questions repeated twice before I grasp what is being asked of me and rarely do I confuse tenses more than ten times in a conversation. My favorite phrase you ask? After the rhetorical “<em>che cazzo fai?</em>” is the much more practical “<em>come si dice</em> [insert English word]<em>?</em>” It’s a thrill watching my tutor drift into daydreams while I search for the ideal word, which is simply any word at all. Any day now, my second passport will be issued under the name <em>Matteo Gilmario</em>.

Progress is slow, to say the least, and mile markers are touchy-feely at best. For example, I would (like to) say my reading and listening skills have improved immensely. At the suggestion of my tutor, I read a novel (<em>Io non ho paura</em>) in Italian and have watched a handful of Italian films <em>senza i sottotitoli</em>. Every day, I read the news in Italian, watch an episode of a show in Italian, and arduously assemble mental sentences in Italian. There’s an unfortunate amount of English to Italian translation happening in my head—I’ve come to believe English is awful to translate because of an over reliance on idiomatic expressions—but those exchanges are beginning to become subconscious. While I can’t quantify my improvements, I am undeniably more proficient in Italian than I was before I began. But how proficient is proficient enough?

To put it simply, I will never be proficient enough to satisfy my ambition, though my answer evinces the underlying question of all hobbies: Why? Why learn a language? Why run marathons? Why build computers from scratch? The <a href="https://www.newyorker.com/culture/open-questions/do-we-need-hobbies" target="_blank">philosophy of hobbying</a> is a good place to start if you’re in search of a pastime,<sup><a id="footnote-2" href="#footnote-2-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;">2</a></sup> and it may provide the necessary structure to separate hobbies (things you genuinely enjoy doing) from interests (things you think you like but might not) from habits (things you do because you do them). Those of us with many hobbies need to prioritize. Realizing, for example, that lifting weights isn’t a source of joy but a recommendation from your doctor that you take seriously is helpful when allocating time for true pleasures.

Language learning falls at a crossroads. At its core, language is a utility—it’s a tool for communication. Here I am, right now, producing language, and here you are, right now, consuming language. (Such is the miracle of language that I can be writing and you can be reading that writing at the same time.) If an activity is strictly utilitarian, it defies the hobby paradigm. Our hobbies ought to exist outside the normal flow of productivity and regimen. The prefixed a- in avocation is an important one: a hobby is denotatively not one’s vocation. To fulfill my linguistic obligation to Italian, my goal in learning the language should be to adequately communicate in Italian, so that, when in Rome, I can do as the Romans do: gesture emphatically and speak Italian at a machine gun clip. If this isn’t my objective and if I don’t achieve it, the whole project feels futile. What’s the point of learning a language if you can't use it?

Well, it’s a hobby. It scratches the itch of intellectual idleness. Therein lies the rub of this particular “hobby” and, for me, hobbies writ large. Where does the hobby end and the function of the skill begin? The separation may appear insignificant—and maybe it is—but I believe it informs how I participate in my hobby. It’s one thing to learn Italian, casually read Italian content, and chat with an Italian tutor. It’s another thing to be standing face-to-face with a man named Lorenzo from Firenze, asking for recommendations on the best gelato in the area. The former is presumably a source of pleasure (or, at the very least, satisfying in some way), the other is an application of a skill. Hobbies are the end and the means, which is a funny way of saying there is no end. There’s only the hobby.

I’m wringing my hands on the particulars of the verbiage because Italian troubles me so. Overall, I’m pleased with my progress,<sup><a id="footnote-3" href="#footnote-3-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;">3</a></sup> but it’s unlikely I’ll make noticeable improvements, specifically regarding oral communication, unless I dramatically increase the amount of time I spend with the language. Every time I forget to switch to the subjunctive, I wince and curse English for omitting the tense (almost) entirely—then I get to pondering. How much time am I willing to invest in nailing the passato prossimo and remembering all the different verbs for “murder”? How much should I expect to improve? What’s the effort-to-expectation ratio?

I could allow myself to enjoy Italian as I enjoy it now.<sup><a id="footnote-4" href="#footnote-4-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;">4</a></sup> Learning, reading, writing, and interpreting road signs on Google Maps. This would mean accepting that I will not achieve bilingual status, at least not any time soon. This will, ostensibly, free up my time and weary brain to become lost in other hobbies and accomplish incredible feats. I have no shortage of pursuits, a few of which I already pursue with much greater vigor than I ever have with Italian. It wouldn’t be unreasonable to suggest that I drop Italian entirely. I have so many hobbies and so many free hours in a day to engage with them. If Italian isn’t a top priority, then maybe it should go.<sup><a id="footnote-5" href="#footnote-5-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;">5</a></sup>

To supplement my Italian education, I’ve been rewatching <em>BoJack Horseman</em>, an adult animated series (emphasis on adult) in Italian. The lead character, BoJack, is an inveterate anti-hero (emphasis on anti) who regularly rewatches episodes of his hit 90s show <em>Horsin’ Around</em>. It’s a run-of-the-mill sit-com, populated with a live studio audience, a kitschy Americana home, and hammy jokes played for perfunctory laughs. He watches the episodes to exorcise his latest demons and remember the glory days before he was consumed by alcoholism and destructive behavior. In his middle age, he’s no longer a star, no longer relevant, and he wants to hold on to that feeling of goodness. It felt good to be on TV, to be rich, to be famous, to have friends and a future. He’s caught up in a vision of himself. Nostalgia is its own kind of drug.

BoJack is unequivocally a bad horseman. His self-pity nostalgia fests are feeble attempts at salvation, to prove to himself, and anyone who’s listening, that he wasn't always an asshole. Most of us don’t have such a checkered past, but we’re prone to bouts of nostalgia all the same for myriad reasons. While we’re on the subject of hobbies, it’s worth mentioning scrapbooking and journaling and photography and poetry, all of which frequently trade on nostalgia or manufacture it. As always, moderation is key. No one wants to be Mr. Peaked In High School or the guy who forgets he was someone else before he became the person he is today. Nostalgia pleases us (remember the good times!) and displeases us (oh god, I want to forget that).

I suffer from a nostalgia-adjacent affliction. I mourn the loss of skill, and it takes on a nostalgic affect. One of the reasons I have so many hobbies—piano, guitar, chess, journaling, photography, and writing, to name a few—is because I refuse to let them go. Once I gain an ability, it’s hard to imagine not having that ability. Not every skill is like riding a bike. In fact, riding a bike may be the exception. (I haven’t ridden a non-stationary bike in a few years, so maybe that’s a lie sold to us by the bike companies.) Once you learn “Clair de Lune” on piano, there’s no guarantee your fingers will know what to do after a few years of neglect. But there shouldn’t be anything wrong with losing a skill, especially if it’s no longer serving any function. And yet, I can’t let go. 

Imagine you're ten years old again. You got a BMX bike and all of your friends have a BMX bike. As a gang of unruly miscreants, you all cruise the neighborhood pretending you’re Shaun White at the X Games. (I know he didn’t ride BMX, lay off.) One day, you pop a wheelie. You’re the first in the crew to pull it off. Now you’re the kid who can do a wheelie. Everyone is asking you to do wheelies so that you’re between an on-demand circus clown and the coolest kid on the block. Doing a wheelie is part of your identity. Who would you be without a wheelie?

Who would I be if I stopped studying Italian? I’m the guy who studies Italian. Maybe it’s in vain, but when you’re an adult, they really do give As for effort. When you leave a pursuit, you’re leaving behind your present skill and giving up future skill. Those skills are part of your identity, both in how you create that identity and how others perceive it. We choose hobbies the way we choose what vinyls to buy, what clothes to wear, how we style our hair. Ideally, there is enjoyment, but there’s room for negotiation. Women wear heels, men get leg extension surgery. We do a lot to construct our self-mythologies in reality.

Part of my identity is my hobbying. I often catch myself saying “I found a cool Wikipedia page…” or “I read a <em>New Yorker</em> article…” or “Let’s see what’s playing at AMC this week.” Others pick up on your hobbies, they inquire about them to learn about you and so they can get you a holiday gift to support your passions. It’s the logical replacement for “What’s your major?” during our college days. We collectively believe that our leisure time activities offer some view into our personhood. Perhaps more so in the past decade, as jobs become de-centered from our lives.<sup><a id="footnote-6" href="#footnote-6-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;">6</a></sup> Being someone who knits suggests a personality or a temperament. It’s a conversation starter, a commonality to expand upon. Asking someone about their hobbies is Small Talk 102—the prerequisite, Small Talk 101, covers weather musings and how crazy it is that it’s the day that it is today.

Hobbies are fundamental to our modern existence, which is increasingly about maximizing one’s free time. Once you clock out for the day, it’s You Time. Express yourself with your white river rafting or oil landscape painting. Try expressing yourself in a new language. It’s harder than it looks, but worth the trouble. At least, I’m telling myself that’s the case. There’s so much to do and so little time to do it. It’s harder than it sounds to be intentional about how you spend your time. But I think making a choice is worth it. <em>Sono contento della mia scelta</em>.{END}
