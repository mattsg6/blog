During summer break, in the halcyon days of pre-middle school childhood, my sisters and I would do the weekly shopping with our mom. Grocery shopping isn’t an 8 year old’s idea of a good time, but the trips are remembered fondly because we were permitted to stroll the toy aisle, our grubby hands hovering over the plastic coverings that were not to be touched. Occasionally, we could claim a toy and we often went for a Webkinz stuffed animal. Those furry friends came with a code that unlocked a virtual friend on <a href="https://www.webkinz.com/" target="_blank">webkinz.com</a>, with whom you could build a Sims-adjacent life, replete with home management, pet care, and, somewhat bafflingly, gem mining. Arte Fact, the slyly named proprietor of the in-game shop, used to wish us well, saying, “Hope you find what you’re looking for,” and I daresay we—Gen Z kids participating in the fad of the day—found exactly what we were looking for.

If memory serves, we were looking for fun. Or, like cartoon heroes Phineas and Ferb, we were merely watching the hours tick by before school started and our lives would be irrevocably destroyed—until our rebirth next June. In my estimation, we were the last generation not to be weaned on the internet. Before proliferation, our summer days were full of intense play, much of it outside, across the neighborhood, through crawlspaces and backyards, in the swamp, on the swingset, under the basketball hoop, sunburnt and sweaty, wrapped up in games whose rules constantly evolved, often towards a baroque structure that mirrored our vision of adult society. (I remember erecting a minibar beneath a friend’s porch and serving Capri-Suns to the regulars.) 

Years passed, friend groups splintered into trios, then pairs. We got phones. We no longer spent our time outside, but squeezed in an hour of video games before camp, rehearsal, practice, or—God forbid—work. We were less addicted to Call of Duty than most Instagram users of today, though we were predisposed, if not pre-addicted, to spend free time literally twiddling our thumbs in front of a screen. Call it destiny. Or call it Webkinz.

My earliest memories of the internet coalesce around a few vibrantly designed interfaces meant to entertain children with trivial games and world exploration. They weren’t pretty and neither was anything else online. Eons have passed in Internet Time. Obviously, the technology has improved immensely—so much so, in fact, that Adobe Flash, the engine behind webkinz.com and many other popular games, was <a href="https://www.adobe.com/products/flashplayer/end-of-life-alternative.html" target="_blank">deprecated in 2021</a>—and UI/UX (design and experience) has become slick and seamless, optimized so that anyone anywhere can load HTML and Javascript in milliseconds. It’s led to a moment of sameness.

Brands (aka everything on the internet) want to appeal to larger, broader markets, which means less personalization, more monotone luxury, and no more ugly fun. Even before ad-festooned pages were de rigour, there was a lot of unpleasantness on an average tour around the pathways of the cyberworld. Looking at the first iterations of Facebook or Twitter is dumbfounding. This is what took over the world? Boxes of text neatly lined up in columns? When TikTok came out, it was pretty much TikTok already. Why waste resources on something that won’t sell when lucrative models already exist? It’s true of all the most prominent tech. Nothing can change too much lest it ruffle the feathers of the masses, so new versions and new products are just past ‘No’ on the ‘Is This Thing Actually New?’ spectrum. Innovation persists, though what these companies are working towards is elusive. The if-it-ain’t-broke-don’t-fix-it philosophy doesn’t fly with stock holders, apparently.

Today, I can’t help but think about Webkinz with a hearty chuckle. The premise of the enterprise—a physical toy that has a digital counterpart—sounds quaint in a world where gaming consoles, like XBox and Playstation, no longer include inputs for discs because the entire experience is contained within a computer. There were two sides of Webkinz playtime: physical play driven by imagination with an actual toy, and online play structured by the (imaginary) rules of a few software developers. It’s hard to recall any connection that Webkinz attempted to bridge between the two, beyond the little guy in your hand resembling the little guy on the screen. When I logged off the site, my golden lab named Barky was fast asleep in his digital bed inside his digital house and the blonde pup in my bedroom was available for fun. It would be weird if I denied myself the ever-alert, corporeal Barky because he was asleep in another dimension.<a id="footnote-1" href="#footnote-1-text" style="text-decoration: none !important;"><sup>1</sup></a>

Webkinz proposed a model of online materialism, transferring value from tangible commodities to, well, intangible commodities. I could brush my stuffed dog’s fur and give him a puka shell necklace, but what good is it if he doesn’t have that same shit in <em>his</em> reality? Granted, I wasn’t thinking like that while strolling around the Webkinz world, desperately pick-axing my way through the mines in search of currency, but the evolution of baseball card trading to Pokemon collecting to Webkinz world building to <a href="https://fortnite.gg/cosmetics?type=outfit" target="_blank">Fortnite skins</a> where players can look like Thanos or Sonic or Indie Navarette in <em>Obsession</em> indicates a slow creep towards a backwards conception of value. Not to put too fine a point on it, but if the things we value aren’t real, what is anything?

Better yet, what is everything? While I’m explaining well-understood cultural phenomena, I might as well add that economic influences are, uh, influential factors in the decisions that guide consumers towards products that are not real. We keep scrolling or playing or staring existentially into the black reflection because of reasons that are enumerated ad nauseum everywhere you look—including the very places that want you to keep looking. The bottomline: the entities on the other side of the wi-fi have our attention and they know that your time (i.e., your attention) is their money. To borrow a phrase from <em>The Social Reckoning</em> by Aaron Sorkin—I believe he’s now writing (and directing) under the nom de plume Captain Obvious—“<a href="https://youtu.be/MfHEZw7Xw_c?t=3" target="_blank">these companies fuckin’ suck.</a>”

This is a solid argument in favor of pitchfork rioting in the name of Ludditedom. Maybe it is time for the Touch Grass Revolution. Or maybe it’s time we use our tech less? Free market capitalists will be pleased to learn that there’s money to be made on the next generation of anti-tech protestors. Apps monitoring app usage, dumb phones, and redirection hobbies are available, at a cost, for those who are firmly against these pernicious forms of technology that usurp our precious time, but still want to have and use the technology to do, presumably, what it was ‘meant to do’: communicate (?). Who’s to say what pocket computers were meant to do—other than put a computer in your pocket.

I’m skeptical of the new doctrinaires. Our lives with easy access to global networks aren’t going anywhere. We are smart homes, not so much programmed to work perfectly, but stitched together like the internet itself to make what was once difficult, difficult in a novel way. Who doesn’t enjoy scrounging around couch cushions for an AirPod and resetting the router because everything relies upon mystical signals floating around us at all times? Though once I find that AirPod and I’m connected to the internet again, I do enjoy the benefits. I like streaming music, watching YouTube, reading Wikipedia, and playing online chess. Productivity, as you well know, is another idea sold to you by office supply marketers (and some other, worse people), so it’s ok to turn the brain off and indulge in free entertainment.

The problem is that the entertainment, at a certain point, isn’t free. Whether it’s sucking up your time or, after an influx of persuasive advertising, has you spending hard earned cash, there is a loss. I’m leery of back-in-my-day talk, but I find myself reflexively agreeing with anyone who says TikTok is dangerous and cell phone usage is a problem. Too much of a good thing, that maybe wasn’t ever good, is bad.

So, again, I have a good laugh thinking of Webkinz, a game that, while entirely online, implied an existence outside of the screen. The object of the stuffed pet made the Webkinz kingdom real when it was anything but. It was like writing to a pen pal. There’s a universe of experiences happening somewhere else and every so often you catch a glimpse. The connection point where Human met Internet was just that: a point. Social media was previously incidental to life. It was a message in a bottle tossed into a pond that your friends had access to—that pond is now an ocean and the bottle is an air raid siren. Life was to be shared online, not lived there.

In 2007, my dad started a blog to share our family’s life online. He used <a href="https://blogger.com" target="_blank">Blogger</a>, a templated blog site owned by Google that’s redolent of the era in internet history when all websites were as colorfully gauche as Webkinz. The Blogger process was, and is, exceedingly simple: an aspiring blogger creates an account, chooses a template, and writes a post. Then another. Before long, a blog is afoot. Unlike Substack, and any other place where opinions are shared, there isn’t a centralized search page on Blogger where trending blogs can be perused alongside up-and-coming blogs and blogs about culture or news or sports or your esoterica of choice. If there is an incredible blogspot (that’s the domain used by Blogger to deploy blogs) about poisonous frogs in Italy, you better hope Google’s Gemini has scanned Blogger thoroughly because there isn’t another way to find it. And if you do find it, you’d be greeted by the sparse HTML of yore: text and pictures and text. Potentially a video, but that’s against the spirit of the medium.

I’m partial to Blogger’s junky minimalism. Blogs by Blogger are highly functionable, which is analogous to “He has a great personality.” The best of Blogger’s output still resembles a one-size-fits-all glove for the internet. It can be blocky, the color palettes can clash. There are no safeguards against seemingly infinite scrolling because of a logorrheic post. Sometimes, the body of the page spills over the X-axis, allowing for a horizontal scroll. It’s Facebook version zero. It’s editing HTML on a Myspace profile. It’s not great. But it doesn’t need to be great. It needs to display text and media. It only needs to be a website.

That said, when I write a post on blogger.com, I get the feeling I’m talking to Google’s unwanted step child. Nothing Google creates is without profit—surely, Blogger provides the company with enough ad revenue to justify its existence—but Blogger is so obviously not a premier product, it borders on neglect. The last post on the community page, called <a href="https://blogger.googleblog.com/" target="_blank">Blogger Buzz</a>, is from May 20, 2020. Anecdotally, there have been updates in the intervening years. No major features or redesigns, however, which means that Google has finally perfected a product or they just don’t care.

According to <a href="https://trends.google.com/explore?q=%2Fm%2F01scs3&date=all&geo=US" target="_blank">Google Trends</a>, interest in Blogger (the website) peaked in 2009. By 2020 it was, evidently, supremely uninteresting. Google doesn’t care because no one else does either. Like so many places on the internet, I would consider disinterest a feature, not a bug. The Blogger situation is ideal in this respect: a major company invests moderate resources into maintenance without overstepping so the core experience isn’t ruined. That sounds pretty good to me.

<a href="#footer">You may have noticed</a> I did not choose Blogger for whatever I’m doing here. Frankly, I thought I could do better. I wanted to house my photography and write screeds that fall into the void. I wanted a place that looked good, but felt (a little) homemade. Substack is in vogue, no surprise considering it’s the natural conclusion to print media’s death and Twitter journalism’s insurgency—it’s easy to post short tirades <em>and</em> write angry missives. For me, it’s too close to the rest of the mess. I’m social media averse at this point and, perhaps, arrogantly contrarian as all 20-somethings ought to be, at least for a while. So, yes, I avoided the mainstream for my own thing. As an American, it’s my right to do it <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w019MzRosmk" target="_blank">my way</a>.

This blog is not a crusade to better the internet or a cry for help. I don’t know what I’m doing and, if my big tech fear mongering didn’t make it clear, I’m skeptical of putting stuff on the internet. <a href="https://youtu.be/6HbrQMgOUFw?t=33" target="_blank">They say it’s written in ink</a>. But I wanted to write more. I wanted to organize my photography. Sharing the fruits of my labor has always applied the necessary pressure to: 1) get the thing done, and 2) put in some real effort. So that’s what I’m doing.

Admittedly, I’m nostalgic for the days I never knew, when writing was for newspapers, books, coffee house poetry, political manifestos, grocery lists, and so on. (Meaning, real things not on the internet.) There’s no sense in being nostalgic for something that wasn’t mine. I grew up on the internet. Playing Webkinz, reading Blogger blogs, watching Vines on YouTube, studying Google Maps, shamelessly cheating my way through academia (that's a joke, Mom), and so much more. I’m still on the internet. (It’s my job.) And, for now, I’m not going anywhere. Where else would I go?{END}
