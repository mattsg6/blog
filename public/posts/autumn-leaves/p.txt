The jazz standard “Autumn Leaves” has, appropriately, been my song of the season. It was composed in 1945 by Joseph Kosma, a twentieth century Hungarian composer, and the lyrics, which were originally in French, were written by Jacque Préverts, a French poet and screenwriter. A few years after its initial release, Johnny Mercer, an American lyricist, translated the song into English, paving the way for the tune to become a true standard if not a member of the Great American Songbook. According to <a href="https://musicbrainz.org/search?query=autumn+leaves&type=work&method=indexed" target="_blank">MusicBrainz</a>, an online encyclopedia of music, there are an astounding 514 recordings of the song. A number like that indicates the post-war music industry was fixated on pop-jazz and, by extension, the country’s appetite for the genre was insatiable.

“Autumn Leaves” is the rule, not the exception, when it comes to this sort of bloat. “Have You Met Miss Jones?” by Rogers and Hart has over 100 recordings, “My Funny Valentine” has nearly 600. Unsurprisingly, popular songs tend to be recorded by the masses, like “Hey Jude,” which boasts some 400 renditions and “I Will Always Love You” around 150. It’s the equivalent of making Spider-Man 3 or 4 or 5 rather than going through the effort of conjuring up a new superhero. Someone else has already done all the work.

But something changed around the turn of the century. In 1999, Cher’s “Believe” topped the year-end charts and has since been recorded over 50 times. A few years later, in 2005, Mariah Carey’s “We Belong Together” ended the year as No. 1, but has only been recorded 11 times, and 7 of those recordings include Mariah Carey. That’s an extreme example—mainstream artists like Adele and Bruno Mars have produced hits that inspired dozens of fresh takes—but contemporary music simply isn’t recycled like it was in the hey-day of the jazz standard. Nowadays, once a song is released, it’s tethered to the artist, like a dog chained to a stake in the front lawn. No one wants to pet that dog. No one wants to record a cover anymore.

There’s a chicken-and-egg problem when trying to discern why exactly this change occurred. Listening habits have, obviously, transformed drastically with the advent of the internet, streaming, and cellphones, which are merely the latest innovations. Before everyone had an AirPod in their ear for most of the day, there were car radios, home radios, record players, gramophones, maybe even a musician sitting down at a piano, or a choir performing in the local town hall. Music was often curated, difficult to obtain, and played out loud for all to hear. Those effects could reasonably be called obstacles—they are challenges to overcome to listen to the music you want to listen to the way you want to listen to it. Innovations on the consumer side of music made it easier to listen to the latest hits in harmonious solitude. Let’s call that the egg part of the problem.

The chicken, then, is the industry. Artists and labels aren’t directing consumers towards one person listening parties (presumably), but there’s no doubt the changing habits of listeners have changed the economics of artistic output. Singles, for example, have steadily increased since music was released. <a href="https://musicbrainz.org/search?query=autumn+leaves&type=work&method=indexed" target="_blank">In 1960</a>, roughly 300 singles were released, in 1985 it was just short of 1,000, and in 2010 the number was over 2,000. It’s linear growth, generally, but it’s largely unfettered, meaning, nothing interrupted this trend for almost a century.FOOTNOTE Streaming platforms reward consistent listening, so artists are compelled to release more music to stay relevant and keep their stats up. Why wouldn’t an artist release more singles?

Perhaps there’s a third party influencing the chicken and its egg. Let’s say hello to the farmer. His name is Apple Music, his friends sometimes call him Spotify, though his mother insists on calling him by his childhood nickname, YouTube. The farmer isn’t too concerned with which came first between the chicken and the egg because he makes so much gosh-darn money that it doesn’t really matter. For those of us who like chicken and eggs, we can’t make the farmer out to be all bad. He has empowered more chickens than ever to produce more eggs than you can possibly imagine. The increase of <s>singles</s> eggs year-over-year isn’t simply because the existing set of <s>artists</s> chickens started producing more. There are more of them, all of whom produce more. It’s genuinely cool that any one of us could tickle the ivories, sing a few bars, and wait patiently for our Grammy to come in the mail because our song is sure to hit billions of streams. That’s all thanks to the farmer.

Animal cruelty is commonplace, however, and we all know it. The farmer knows it but won’t admit it, and we idly stare from the farm gates, muttering half-assed chants of solidarity, while the abuse takes place in broad daylight. Release those chickens! (Okay, I’ll stop the metaphor.) Artists don’t make the big bucks from the platforms that would not exist without them, which isn’t right. The people—that would be you and me—have yet to enact a revolution and it doesn’t appear that one is on the horizon. We love our music too much to quit listening the easy way. Retro methods of listening are making a comeback—what’s old is new—though this ‘resurgence’ is complementary to one’s Daily Mix on Spotify, it’s not meant as a replacement.

I’m not interested in blaming the farmer, the chicken, or the egg. These types of macro-convergences happen all the time and the cause-and-effect relationships often reveal trends in economics, culture, and anything else that falls under the umbrella of society. Underneath all that nonsense, I believe there are unverifiable, and therefore mystical, shadow trends which are subtly influencing our behavior in some non-obvious ways. If you’ll allow me to stretch our poultry metaphor a little further, these unseen effects might have to do with soil quality, noise pollution, cow birth rates, farmers’ standard of living, and so on. What does it mean to live in a world where chickens and their eggs are so easy for us to consume?

What does it mean to live in a world where music is steamed directly from the cloud to our ears? In this country, one whose foundational principle is curiously the “pursuit of happiness,” individualism is a human right, potentially the only one worth protecting. We have the right to listen to music by ourselves, and make it by ourselves. Consider, <a href="https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/bands-solo-artists-music-charts" target="_blank">for example</a>, that the most popular songs of the day are increasingly produced by solo artists or collabs rather than bands, which would indicate that we’re consuming music by lone musicians as lone listeners more than we ever have. How’s that for individual liberty?

Freedom is a word I rarely use without thinking of the times when I’ve been loved, so said Donovan in his folk hit "<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dpUSQNvtzsk&list=RDdpUSQNvtzsk&start_radio=1" target="_blank">Colours</a>" from 1965. Freedom is a word with a lot of mileage in these United States and it’s often conflated with individualism. The right to act as an individual is how many people perceive freedom. By this token, one might say that being a solo artist constitutes more freedom than being in a band, which sounds so obvious as to be a vacuous truth. No shit, having fewer people around to criticize my lyrics and arrangements means I can do what I damn-well please in the studio. Anyone who has played in a band (that they liked) will tell you this isn’t true. Sure, there’s some compromise to make the band work, but creativity flourishes when you can spontaneously riff off your mates in a jam session or painstakingly mix a new song on the one laptop with Adobe software or transcend to higher planes of existence with a kickass take. Lofty aspirations aside, bands don’t have to restrict artist freedom, and often they do the opposite.

Is the same true of listening? Many of the places we frequent are drowned in music—malls, restaurants, elevators, dentist offices, bathrooms, the gym, etc.—but it would be rare to witness a group of people actively listening in one of those contexts. When was the last time you listened to music, and I mean really listened, with other people? Maybe it was in the car and you all belted out “Nessun Dorma” on your way to the symphony. Maybe you’re young and it was a concert where the sound was so loud you felt it in your vibrating rib cage more than you heard it. Suffice to say, it’s likely you listen to music by yourself more often than the alternative. This may have been true for some time, though the ease of listening combined with the mobility of listening devices has created a new phenomenon.

I call it the Soundtrack Fallacy. When we think of a perfect cinematic moment, we think of the <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=isJZt2r_jzg" target="_blank">Death Star exploding</a>, <a href="https://youtu.be/Q_qhLRUh66k?si=q2kL10vjkaz4Zn9k&t=490" target="_blank">Rocky screaming for Adrian</a>, or <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q9JSVGS4dHU" target="_blank">Harry kissing Sally on New Year’s Eve</a>. In the background of all those scenes is a soundtrack. The music supervisor has slaved away for hours to find the ideal song, something that animates the thrill of the climax, pushing the characters and the audience towards exultation. Even in <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nMNCRBSg39g" target="_blank">intimate</a> <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ljMrCkRCZ10" target="_blank">screen</a> <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E_z2PGmUIrE" target="_blank">moments</a>, ones that call for melancholy or tragedy, the score stirs us, directing us to an emotion or deepening what’s already there. These scenes often feel otherworldly, beyond our reality in ways that we can’t understand because we don’t live our lives with the perfect co-star, the perfect shot, or the perfect song paving our path to bliss on a cinematic magnitude. But no matter how loudly we play our music, it won’t make us the star of a movie.

Lately, I have been trying to defy that logic by creating my own soundtracks for my day-to-day. If I take the train, I’m apt to put on Chet Baker and stare plaintively out the window. (It’s most effective in the evening.) When I’m in the car on a sunny day, I like Jim Croce and Dave van Ronk. Going for a walk typically entails a playlist of my most listened to songs of late, though sometimes I’ll go for John Field nocturnes or John Coltrane ballads, whatever might let my mind wander. I activate a mood or gently guide my current one someplace that feels more profound, significant, cinematic. 

Earlier, I mentioned “Autumn Leaves” was my song of the season. My favorite recording of the standard is Bill Evans on his 1959 album <em>Portrait in Jazz</em>. Stylistically, Bill gravitates towards the melancholy, often playing in the middle range of the piano with tight left-hand harmonic structures and a sparseness that suggests a yearning. I always want more of Bill. His “<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r-Z8KuwI7Gc&list=RDr-Z8KuwI7Gc&start_radio=1" target="_blank">Autumn Leaves</a>” is, I think, one of his great triumphs. He takes it at a brisk tempo, striking the keys with his typical water-droplet clarity. His adornments on Scott LaFaro’s bass solo are edging towards zany, but he’s not really that guy—it’s playful and energetic. Importantly, it’s written in a minor key so the underlying engine of the Bill Evans experience—sadness—is intact. When I listen to Bill’s take on the song, I can almost hear the “leaves” in the title turn from a noun to a verb, as if to say: autumn is leaving.

This song makes me feel like I’m in a movie and almost any movie at that. It could be a French crime thriller from the 60s or a particularly surly beginning to a rom-com or an existential drama set in a quiet Boston neighborhood.FOOTNOTE Being in a movie is intoxicating. You feel confident and important, like your mere presence on the sidewalk is worthy of other people’s attention. <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4KlSuGNt8e4" target="_blank">Witness me</a>!

I get the sense I’m not alone in this indulgence. <a href="https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2017/time-with-tunes-how-technology-is-driving-music-consumption/" target="_blank">People</a> are listening to music all the time and the contexts in which they listen to music are growing. What’s more, people like to share what they’re listening to with as many people as possible. It’s incredibly easy to put up a story with a picture of your drying tears while a Max Richter piece plays forlornly in the background, as if your personal tragedy is so precious that not only should other people see it, they should hear it, too, in such exquisite, precise tones. Our lives are lived between our ear(bud)s.

There’s an argument to be made—a tempting one!—that these habits further separate us, desensitizing our exposure to the sounds of the world, which is to say, the sounds of each other. Is there value in the sound of your tires on asphalt or the grinding of a train on rails? Should we pay more attention to the coughs, sneezes, farts, and belches of our fellow humans? What can we learn by eavesdropping on the salacious conversation of two friends catching up after a long day? It’s possible we are too caught up in ourselves, oblivious to the banal beauties and horrors of everything around us, while we disassociate in the espionage caper we dreamed up when listening to “Skyfall.”

I don’t think it does anyone any good to be maudlin about these cultural shifts in entertainment consumption. Shit happens! There’s no denying that I enjoy plugging into my playlist, not just because it makes the commute or workout better, but because I like music. There’s also no denying that pumping music directly into your brain is, well, a little dangerous. Besides the biological damage it may be exacting, it likely makes us less aware, more susceptible to the terrors of our world, and possibly more like an asshole movie star who thinks everything is about him.

But I want it wherever I go—not all the time, but a lot of the time. It can be pleasant to take off the headphones and listen for signs of life. I hear a window rolling down and a rageful driver haranguing a pedestrian. I hear friends laughing. I cover my mouth because I hear an old guy attempting a world record for most phlegm produced on a public bus. And this time of year I hear the susurrus of leaves across the barren soil. And so autumn leaves. I think I hear winter coming around the corner.FOOTNOTE{END}
